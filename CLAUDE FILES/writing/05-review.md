# Stage 5: Review

Take the draft to final. Run every mechanical check, cut what fails, and confirm the doc still moves the reader from start state to end state. This stage does not add ideas; it removes what does not earn its place and fixes what reads wrong.

Needs the draft (Stage 4), the reader profile (Stage 1), `00-voice-reference.md`, `00-craft-principles.md`, and `00-line-principles.md`. Owns the final check on craft principles 7 and 9 (every plant pays off, the parts readers skip are gone) and the line-level pass.

One rule outranks every check here. When a rule fights clarity, clarity wins (the rule above the rules in `00-line-principles.md`). A flagged passive, a long word, a long sentence, or a broken pattern that serves the reader better than the "correct" form stays. A critic that demands the rule over the clearer result is wrong. Every flag is a question, not a verdict; the test is always whether the change lowers the reader's uncertainty.

## How to do it

Read every sentence against the one test: did it lower the reader's uncertainty, could the reader have predicted it, is it here for the reader or the writer. Fails all three, cut it. Fails two, rewrite it. Fails one, weigh the one job against the space.

Run the banned-word and structural-pattern lists from the voice reference against the full text. Replace inflated words with plain ones. Cut filler, hedges, and intensifiers. Surface every passive and every comma-but for a keep-or-rewrite call.

Run the counts from the voice reference. Flag sentences over 30 words, sentences with more than two commas, paragraphs over five sentences, walls of text, monotone runs, and openings that repeat the same first word. Fix each flag.

Check that every recomputable number reconciles to the precision shown. Do the subtraction yourself. If the table says 4.33 and 3.98 and the text claims 0.36, either show more decimals or correct the figure.

Walk the plants and payoffs one last time, principle 7. Every conspicuous detail the draft raised should pay off, and every payoff should trace to a plant. Cut the dangling plant; seed the unplanted payoff. A draft can read clean sentence by sentence and still leave a promise unkept.

Find the parts the reader skips, principle 9. Mark any paragraph you would skim if a stranger wrote it: a run-up before the real claim, dawdling after the point lands. Cut them. Watch the opposite failure too, where a cut ran so deep the reader cannot follow how one section reached the next.

Check the first line against what follows, line principle 9. The lead makes a promise; confirm the piece delivers it and the lead does not oversell. Check the near-cousin words, line principle 3: where a word almost fits, see whether a more exact one says the precise degree. These two are not on the banned-word list, so the mechanical critics miss them.

For a persuasive piece, judge it the way `00-persuasion-principles.md` does: by whether the audience would act, not by whether the writing impressed (persuasion rule 8). Confirm three things. The piece asks for exactly one action and makes it impossible to miss, with the cost of yes kept low (rule 10); two competing asks mean the reader does neither. Every claim carries proof, and no claim outruns what the proof can back (rule 9). And run the meta-pattern check: if the piece feels weak, look first at whether it is specific, whether it meets the audience where their belief sits, and whether that audience wanted the outcome at all, since most failures trace to those three before anything tactical.

Confirm the doc is self-contained. No personal host, path, or name the reader cannot use. No internal variable name where a plain word works. Sources linked, not narrated.

Re-read against the reader profile last. Does the doc carry every load-bearing input and result? Does it still skip what the reader already knows? A doc can pass every word-level check and still fail because it dropped a fact the reader needs, or explained something the reader knows. Concision and completeness are both failures when out of balance.

## Adversarial pass

Spawn parallel critics, each owning a slice so nothing is checked by only one pass:

1. **Banned-word and inflation critic.** Hunt the banned list and the inflated-vocabulary table. Report every hit with its replacement.
2. **Counts critic.** Run every measurement in the voice reference. Report each sentence or paragraph that trips a threshold.
3. **Uncertainty critic.** Apply the three-question test to every sentence. Report the ones that fail and which test they failed.
4. **Reconciliation and self-containment critic.** Recompute every number the reader could check. Find every personal path, host, name, or internal identifier. Report what does not tie out or does not travel.
5. **Completeness critic.** Hold the draft against the reader profile. What load-bearing input or result is missing? What does the draft explain that the reader already knows? Report both directions.
6. **Structure critic.** Walk the draft for the two structural failures that survive to the end: a plant that never pays off or a payoff with no plant (principle 7), and a part the reader would skip or a cut so deep the thread breaks (principle 9). Report each with the sentence and its missing other half.
7. **Lead-and-word critic.** Does the first line promise what the piece delivers, without overselling (line principle 9)? Where does a near-cousin word miss the exact meaning a more precise one would catch (line principle 3)? Report the lead that does not pay off and the word that is close but wrong.
8. **Action-and-proof critic** (for a piece that must move the audience to act). Does the piece ask for exactly one action, obvious and low-cost (persuasion rule 10)? Does every claim carry proof, with none outrunning what the proof backs (rule 9)? Run the meta-pattern: is it specific, does it meet the audience's awareness, was there demand. Report competing asks, an unproven or overreaching claim, and any of the three meta-pattern failures.

Every critic applies the clarity override: do not report a rule-break that reads clearer than the rule would. Flag it only when the rule serves the reader better than the break.

A synthesizer merges the flags into one edit list, ordered by severity. Apply the edits. If the completeness critic found a missing fact, that is a substance gap; fix it even though it adds words. The doc is done when it survives all five with nothing left to cut and nothing missing.
