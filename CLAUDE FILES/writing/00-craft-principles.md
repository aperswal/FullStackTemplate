# Craft Principles

Ten rules for planning and structure. The voice reference governs how a sentence reads; these govern whether the thing as a whole holds together. Some are universal (a doc and a novel both need a whole, causality, setup and payoff). Some are narrative-first (emotional arc, the man with a gun) and apply to long-form and story more than to a one-page memo. Each rule names the stage that owns it.

## 1. Decide first: architect or gardener

"There are architects and gardeners." — George R.R. Martin

Architects blueprint the whole structure before the first nail; gardeners plant a premise and water it, finding the shape as it grows. Most writers blend the two. The mistake is fighting your own nature.

Do:
- Run the pipeline strictly in order if you are an architect: outline locks before drafting.
- Start with a situation and a question, not a map, if you are a gardener: draft the hot section first, backfill the structure after.
- Lean hybrid: architect the load-bearing parts (the ending, the argument's logic) and garden the discovery-heavy parts (voice, the example that surprises you).

Don't:
- Don't adopt a method because someone you admire uses it. Both kinds finish books.
- Don't confuse "no outline" with "no thought." Gardeners still know what seed they planted.

Good:
- (Architect) A methodology writeup charts every input, the calculation, and the validation before a sentence, because the reader's trust depends on an airtight chain.
- (Gardener) A retrospective starts with one moment that stuck, follows what it implies, and lets the structure emerge from what the moment turns out to mean.
- (Hybrid) A proposal fixes the ask and the three load-bearing claims first, then drafts the supporting sections in whatever order they come alive.

Bad:
- (Gardener forced to architect) A natural discoverer forces a rigid outline because a guide demanded it, then cannot write, because the discovery already happened on the outline.
- (Architect refusing to plan) A natural planner trusts the process, writes deep into a corner, and abandons the piece because nothing connects.
- (Rigidity) A writer outlines so tightly that when a better structure reveals itself mid-draft, they shove it back into the plan and the piece goes flat.

## 2. Give it a beginning, a middle, and an end

"A whole is that which has a beginning, a middle, and an end." — Aristotle

A piece is a complete action, not a pile of incidents. The beginning sets something loose, the middle moves it, the end resolves what the middle set going. Nothing essential missing, nothing extra bolted on.

Do:
- State the single thing the piece completes in one sentence. If you cannot, the structure is not whole yet.
- Make the ending answer the question the beginning asked.
- Cut sections that do not feed the central action, or promote them until they do.

Don't:
- Don't mistake stuff happening for a middle. Sections you could reorder without consequence are not a structure.
- Don't end on something the beginning never promised.

Good:
- (Setup to payoff) A doc opens with a question to decide, spends the middle on the evidence, ends on the decision the evidence forces.
- (Small scope, still whole) A one-page update: the change, what it broke, what it now does.
- (Question asked and answered) A proposal asks in the first line whether to fund the work; every middle section shifts the odds; the last line makes the call.

Bad:
- (No end) A writeup with a gripping setup and a sprawling middle that stops because the author ran out of room, not because the action completed.
- (No beginning) A doc that opens mid-detail with no sense of what is at stake.
- (No whole) An "and then, and then" report where the sections sit in any order and add up to nothing.

## 3. Build on causality, not just sequence

"The king died and then the queen died of grief." — E.M. Forster

"The king died, then the queen died" is sequence. Add "of grief" and it is structure: events bound by cause. Sequence makes a reader ask "and then?"; causality makes them ask "why?", the stronger pull.

Do:
- Link each beat to the one before with "therefore" or "but." If only "and then" fits, the link is weak.
- Draw arrows when you storyboard, not just a list.
- Let consequences accumulate. A good structure remembers itself and pays off earlier causes later.

Don't:
- Don't string sections by chronology alone.
- Don't let coincidence carry the load. It can start trouble; it is suspect when it solves trouble.

Good:
- (Tight chain) A postmortem where the outage happened because of the skipped check, which happened because of the deadline, each link causing the next.
- (Cause across the arc) An argument where the recommendation in the last section follows from the constraint named in the first.
- (Long-range payoff) A design doc where an early choice returns as the reason a later tradeoff is forced.

Bad:
- (Pure sequence) A status doc: Monday X, Tuesday Y, with no link but the calendar.
- (Unconnected resolution) A conclusion that arrives with no chain the reader could have followed.
- (Coincidence as engine) A result that holds only because a number happened to land right, with no reason the reader can trace.

## 4. Map the emotional arc

"Somebody gets into trouble, gets out of it again." — Kurt Vonnegut

Vonnegut graphed stories on fortune over time and found the loved ones trace simple shapes. Storyboard the feeling, not just the facts. For non-narrative work, the arc is the reader's conviction: doubt to belief, or question to answer, with a low point before the turn.

Do:
- Sketch the line. Where does the reader's tension or the subject's fortune rise and fall? A flat line is a flat piece.
- Put the low point before the climb, and land the end somewhere different from the start.
- Use the shape to find dead stretches. A long span with no change is a section to cut or charge.

Don't:
- Don't hold the level flat for sections at a time.
- Don't end at the height you started, unless flatness is the point.

Good:
- (Man in hole) A case study that dives (the thing broke), bottoms out (the cost), and climbs above the start (the fix made it better than before).
- (Deliberate descent) A risk memo charted as a steady fall, the shape itself refusing false hope.
- (Reversal) A doc that sets an expectation, drops it (the obvious approach fails), then lands higher (the approach that works).

Bad:
- (Flat line) The subject is fine, stays fine, wins easily, ends fine. Nothing earned.
- (Monotone bottom) An all-doom piece with no rise or fall, where the alarm goes numb.
- (Front-loaded) A strong open and a long eventless plateau that reveals the back half is dead weight.

## 5. Write toward the ending

"I'll begin where I always begin, which is at the end." — John Irving

Knowing the destination lets every earlier section point at it, and turns foreshadowing into a by-product. Decide the final image or line before drafting, even if everything else changes.

Do:
- Fix the ending first. It is the compass. (This is the end state from Stage 1.)
- Audit each section against it: does this bend toward that finish?
- Let the known ending plant honest setup. It is easy to seed when you know what is coming.

Don't:
- Don't wander hoping an ending appears. That is how middles bloat.
- Don't love the destination so much you ignore a better one the draft reveals.

Good:
- (Earned close) A doc that ends on a specific decision seeds the exact constraint behind it in the first section.
- (Fair clues) An argument that fixes its conclusion first, then includes only the evidence that conclusion needs.
- (Voice from the end) A piece whose last line sets the tone for the whole.

Bad:
- (No destination) A draft that reaches its climax and finds the setup supports no satisfying finish.
- (Stubborn destination) An ending locked so hard that when the evidence points elsewhere, the planned conclusion stays and the piece rings false.
- (Broken promise) Heavy setup toward an ending the author later drops, leaving early promises unpaid.

## 6. Work in scene cards you can rearrange

"The pattern of the thing precedes the thing." — Vladimir Nabokov

The unit of planning is the section, and sections move. Write the one you are hot for, wherever it falls. Sequencing problems are obvious only when you can see and move the pieces.

Do:
- Put each section or beat on its own card.
- Lay them out and physically reorder them.
- Lock the order only once the full set feels right.

Don't:
- Don't force front-to-back if a later section is burning to be written now.
- Don't treat the first order as permanent. Rearranging is the point.

Good:
- (Gap fills itself) A writer stuck on the setup writes the result first, then sees the setup exists only to reach it.
- (See the spacing) Forty cards on the floor reveal two reveals landing back to back; one slides earlier.
- (Only the needed sections) Drafting the open and close first shows exactly which connective sections the gap demands, and no filler.

Bad:
- (No visible shape) Everything kept in the head, sections never compared, three doing the same job.
- (Cards but no moves) Carded but never reordered, so the cards are a to-do list and the structural problems stay hidden.
- (Pieces, no picture) Brilliant fragments that never resolve into a whole.

## 7. Plant it, then pay it off

"If it's not going to be fired, it shouldn't be hanging there." — Anton Chekhov

Draw attention to something and it must matter later; what matters later should usually be planted earlier. Setup and payoff, and the discipline of cutting decoration that pays off nothing.

Do:
- Track promises. For every conspicuous detail, note where it pays off; for every payoff, note where it was planted.
- Plant early and quietly so the payoff feels earned.
- If a planted element never pays off, pay it off or cut it.

Don't:
- Don't lavish attention on something that goes nowhere.
- Don't pull a decisive solution out of nowhere with no setup.

Good:
- (Fact) A throwaway line that the data only covers H2 becomes the reason a later claim is hedged.
- (Definition) A term defined up front is the exact one the result leans on later.
- (Constraint) A limit named early returns as the reason a later option is ruled out.

Bad:
- (Setup, no payoff) A doc dwells on a caveat for three sections, then never uses it. The reader feels cheated.
- (Payoff, no setup) A conclusion rests on a fact never introduced. The reader cannot follow.
- (Over-planting) The foreshadowing is so heavy the twist is obvious long before it lands.

## 8. Decide what to leave underwater

"Anything you know you can eliminate, and it only strengthens your iceberg." — Ernest Hemingway

If you truly know the submerged part, you can leave it off the page and the reader still feels its weight. Omission is a planning decision: know far more than you show.

Do:
- Build the full backstory and detail in your notes, then choose what stays submerged.
- Imply through action and specifics; trust the reader to feel what you left out.
- Cut explanation the structure already conveys.

Don't:
- Don't omit what you do not actually know. That leaves a hole, not depth.
- Don't withhold so much that the piece becomes confusing instead of resonant.

Good:
- (Detail submerged) A doc references the migration once and never explains it; the offhandedness makes the system feel large and real.
- (Reasoning submerged) A recommendation states the call and the one fact behind it, leaving the ten supporting analyses in the appendix.
- (Confidence from omission) The pipeline is described in five steps because the author knows the fifty underneath.

Bad:
- (Confusing omission) So much is withheld the reader cannot tell what the piece is about.
- (Hollow omission) The author skips a reason not by design but because they never worked it out; the gap reads as a hole, because it is.
- (Over-explaining) Every point spelled out, the whole iceberg on the surface, weightless.

## 9. Cut the parts readers skip

"Try to leave out the part that readers tend to skip." — Elmore Leonard

Readers skim throat-clearing, long setup, and idle preamble. They do not skip the result and the turn. Plan to enter late, leave early, and delete the connective filler.

Do:
- Mark the part of each section that is just getting to the point, and start after it.
- Get in as late as possible and out as soon as the point lands.
- In revision, ask which paragraphs you would skim if someone else wrote them. Cut those.

Don't:
- Don't open with scene-setting for atmosphere alone; readers leaf ahead for the substance.
- Don't write long "and so we did the setup" bridges; cut to the next thing that matters.

Good:
- (Late entry) A section that once opened with preamble now opens on the first real claim.
- (Hard cut) A doc jumps from the decision straight to its consequences, trusting the reader to skip the deliberation.
- (End on the point) A section ends on the result, not a paragraph mulling it.

Bad:
- (Atmosphere-only open) Three paragraphs of background before a single substantive sentence.
- (Post-decision dawdling) A page of second-guessing after the call is obvious.
- (Over-cutting) So much connective tissue removed that the reader cannot tell how one section led to the next. Compression became confusion.

## 10. When a scene dies, raise the stakes

"...have a man come through the door with a gun in his hand." — Raymond Chandler

Chandler's half-joke for a stalling story. A section gone slack usually needs more pressure, not more words. Take it as a diagnostic, not a literal instruction.

Do:
- When a section flatlines, ask what would raise the stakes for the reader right now, and do that.
- Use it to diagnose: if a jolt would genuinely help, the real problem is the section has no tension or goal.
- Find the version of "the gun" the piece allows: a sharper claim, a number, a consequence, a deadline, a counterexample.

Don't:
- Don't reach for a literal shock every time. Manufactured drama papers over a structural hole.
- Don't add a threat with no consequence later. That is noise, and it breaks rule 7.

Good:
- (Reframed as a number) A vague section comes alive when the cost it implies is stated in dollars.
- (Reframed as a consequence) A flat explanation ignites when it names what breaks if the reader ignores it.
- (Reframed as a stake) A meandering setup gets a jolt when it names the deadline the work is racing.

Bad:
- (Drama as filler) A shocking aside that changes nothing in the piece.
- (Symptom ignored) A jolt added to fix a boring section, with no question of why it was boring, so the next section stalls the same way.
- (No setup, no aftermath) A sudden claim raised and dropped, never connected to anything.
