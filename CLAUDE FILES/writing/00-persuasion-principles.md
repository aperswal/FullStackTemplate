# Persuasion Principles

Ten rules from direct-response copywriting. This is the persuasion-to-action lens, and it applies when the piece has to hook an audience and move them to do one thing: marketing copy, a landing page, a cold email, a Slack ask, a pitch, a proposal that needs a yes. It applies least to reference material and to docs for a captive audience who will read regardless.

This set carries one thing the others do not: a layer that sits before the writing. Whether an audience already wants the outcome (rule 5) and where their belief already sits (rule 3) are decided in Stage 1, before a word is drafted. The rest is tactical and lives in Stages 2, 4, and 5.

Much of the tactical layer overlaps the line and screen sets. The map says which rules add something new and which restate a rule you already have from the persuasion angle, so no critic checks the same thing twice.

## 1. Spend most of your effort on the headline

"When you have written your headline, you have spent eighty cents out of your dollar." — David Ogilvy

The headline decides whether the rest gets read. It must carry a complete hook on its own.

Do: Write 10 to 20 headlines before choosing one. Make it promise, intrigue, or call out the exact reader.
Don't: Treat it as a label or an afterthought, or be clever at the cost of clarity.

Good:
- (Benefit promise) "Pay off your mortgage 7 years early without earning a dollar more." Leads with the transformation.
- (Curiosity gap) "The $4 vitamin most cardiologists take but rarely mention." Opens a loop the reader must close.
- (Specific and targeted) "For freelancers who hate chasing invoices: get paid in 48 hours." Names the reader and the payoff.

Bad:
- (Clever pun, no promise) "Sole Mates" for a shoe store. Cute, says nothing.
- (Vague generic) "Quality you can trust." True of everything, owned by no one.
- (About you, not them) "Welcome to Acme Financial Solutions." A sign on a door, not a reason to read on.

## 2. Be specific, kill generalities

"Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." — Claude Hopkins

Specifics feel true and double as proof. This is the line set's "be concrete" rule and the screen set's "show don't tell" from the selling angle. Do not run a separate check; those own it. Ogilvy proved a Rolls-Royce was quiet not by calling it quiet but by the sound of the dashboard clock.

Good: exact figure ("cuts reporting from 6 hours to 19 minutes"), concrete mechanism ("a ceramic layer three atoms thick"), named dated proof ("used by 1,214 dental offices since March 2019").
Bad: vague quantifier ("helps tons of businesses"), empty superlative ("the best software, period"), suspiciously round and hedged ("save up to 50%").

## 3. Enter the conversation already in their mind

"Always enter the conversation already taking place in the customer's mind." — Robert Collier

Start where the reader already is: their current belief, frustration, and level of awareness. Use their words, not yours. This is new, and it sharpens the Stage 1 audience profile: "already knows" is not just facts, it is how aware they are of the problem and the solution.

Do: Meet the reader at their exact stage and echo the language they use.
Don't: Begin from your starting point, re-sell people already sold, or assume knowledge they lack.

Good:
- (Problem-aware) "Still waking up at 3 a.m. with your mind racing? Here's what's going on."
- (Solution-aware, undecided) "You've already tried melatonin. Here's why it stops working, and what does."
- (Their objection flipped) "I don't have time to meal-prep" becomes "Dinner's on the table in 11 minutes. No prep."

Bad:
- (Too early) Explaining what email marketing is to a CMO who already buys it.
- (Too late) Opening a beginner's ad with "leverage our omnichannel attribution stack."
- (Ignores the real objection) Hammering discounts when the reader's fear is whether it works at all.

## 4. Sell the sizzle, not the steak

"Sell the sizzle, not the steak." — Elmer Wheeler

People buy what a thing does for them and how it makes them feel, not the spec sheet. New for docs: translate every feature into what it gets the reader, and push to the benefit behind the benefit.

Do: Translate every feature into a benefit, and push to the benefit behind it.
Don't: List specs and assume the reader connects the dots. Don't stop at the shallow payoff.

Good:
- (Feature to benefit) "256-bit encryption" becomes "your bank details stay yours, even on hotel Wi-Fi."
- (Benefit of the benefit) "Get your evenings back" becomes "so you make it to your kid's games again."
- (Identity) "Walk into the room as the person who clearly has it handled."

Bad:
- (Spec dump) "4.7-inch OLED, 128GB, IP68, A17 chip."
- (Feature stated as self-evidently good) "Now with blockchain."
- (Benefit too generic) "Helps you be more productive." Productive at what, and so what?

## 5. Find a starving crowd

"The only advantage I want is a starving crowd." — Gary Halbert

Demand beats wording. Pick an audience that already wants the result; the words come second. This is strategy, decided in Stage 1 before drafting, and it is the meta-pattern's root cause: most failing pieces aimed at a crowd that was never hungry.

Do: Aim at people already hurting or actively looking for the outcome.
Don't: Try to manufacture demand for something nobody seeks, however good the words.

Good: urgent recurring pain (tax software to freelancers in March), a rising trend you ride, proven buyers already paying for a worse option.
Bad: demand from scratch for a problem no one feels, an audience with no budget authority, an indifferent market nobody loses sleep over.

For a doc, the parallel is real: a proposal lands when it answers a question the reader is already asking. A brilliant doc on a problem leadership does not feel goes unread no matter how it reads.

## 6. Make it simple and conversational

"Make it simple. Make it memorable. Make it inviting to look at." — Leo Burnett

Short words, short sentences, one idea at a time. Write like you would say it aloud to one person. The voice reference and line set already own this; not re-checked here.

Good: short and concrete ("it snaps shut, you hear the click, done"), conversational rhythm, one idea per sentence.
Bad: corporate jargon, inflated vocabulary, a 40-word run-on with three subordinate clauses.

## 7. Make every line pull them to the next

"Slippery slide." — Joseph Sugarman. The only job of the first sentence is to get the second sentence read.

Build momentum so stopping feels harder than continuing. This is the screen "make me care" and the line "lead is a promise" from the momentum angle; those own it. Open short, intriguing, easy. Design each sentence to leak into the next.

Good: short punchy opener ("I almost didn't write this"), an open curiosity loop, a provocative question.
Bad: throat-clearing ("in today's fast-paced world"), company history first, a nine-line wall of text.

## 8. Treat copy as salesmanship, not art

"Advertising is salesmanship in print." — John E. Kennedy

The piece exists to get a result, not applause. Judge it by response, not by how clever it sounds. New as a judging standard for Stage 5: the test is whether the reader acts, not whether the writing impressed.

Do: Make the case clear, give reasons to believe, handle the obvious objection, ask for the action.
Don't: Be clever for its own sake, chase admiration, or entertain without making the case.

Good: reason-why ("cheaper because we skipped the middleman, not the materials"), objection handled ("worried about switching? we move your data in one call"), demonstration over adjectives.
Bad: a witty headline no one can tie to the product, a gorgeous metaphor that leaves the reader unsure what is for sale, a fun story with no offer and no next step.

## 9. Back every claim with proof, tell the truth

"The most powerful element in advertising is the truth." — Bill Bernbach

The more believable a claim, the more it persuades. Proof beats adjectives. This deepens the storyboard's evidence rule: every claim names the proof that backs it, and a claim that outruns its proof reads as a lie.

Do: Support claims with specifics, demonstrations, testimonials, data, or guarantees.
Don't: Stack unprovable superlatives or hype that trips the reader's BS detector.

Good: specific named testimonial, demonstration with the actual numbers, risk reversal ("60 days, hate it and we refund every cent").
Bad: vague testimonial ("great product, highly recommend"), unsupported superlative ("the #1 most revolutionary breakthrough in history"), a claim proof cannot back ("lose 30 pounds in 10 days, guaranteed"), which reads as a lie.

## 10. Drive one single, clear, frictionless action

Core of the direct-response tradition (Caples, Wunderman): a confused reader never acts. Every piece earns exactly one next step.

New for docs: name the single thing the reader should do, make it impossible to miss, tell them what happens after, and lower the cost of saying yes. The single ask is decided in Stage 1 (it is the end state) and shaped in Stage 2 (where it sits).

Do: Ask for exactly one action, make it obvious, reduce the cost of yes.
Don't: Offer competing actions, hide the ask, or make the first step too big.

Good: one specific action ("start your free 14-day trial, no card"), tells what happens next ("enter your email and I'll send the template in 2 minutes"), risk removed.
Bad: competing CTAs ("buy now, or read the blog, or follow us"), a vague ask ("learn more"), too big too soon (a 12-month contract before any trust).

## The meta-pattern

Audit failing copy and most of it breaks rule 2 (be specific), rule 3 (enter the conversation), or rule 5 (the crowd was never hungry). The rest are usually downstream of those three. Headlines, sizzle, and CTAs are the tactical layer; awareness, specificity, and market are the layer that decides whether the tactics get a chance. When a persuasive piece is failing, check those three first.
