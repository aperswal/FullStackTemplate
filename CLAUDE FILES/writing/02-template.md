# Stage 2: Template

Turn the reader profile into a section skeleton. This picks the shape of the document before any prose exists. The skeleton is section headers plus a one-line note on what each will hold, nothing more.

Needs the reader profile from Stage 1.

Owns principles 2, 5, and 6 in `00-craft-principles.md`: the piece needs a beginning, middle, and end; the skeleton points at the ending fixed in Stage 1; sections are cards you lay out and reorder before locking.

## What to produce

An ordered list of section headers. For each, one line on what it will contain. No prose yet.

## How to do it

Pick sections so each builds on the one before, the way a proof builds on its prerequisites. The reader should never need a fact from a later section to understand an earlier one. If section 4 needs a definition, that definition lives in section 1 or 2, not in a footnote after the fact.

Order by what the reader needs first. A reader deciding whether to fund something needs the result and the ask early; the methodology that backs it can come after. A reader who will run the pipeline needs the inputs before the steps.

Lead with the outcome when the doc carries one. Definitions and setup come after the reader knows why they are reading. For a piece meant to move a reader rather than serve as reference, this is screen principle 9 in `00-screen-principles.md`: earn the reader's investment before the mechanics. Open on the stakes, the problem that bites them, or the result that matters, so the reader has a reason to follow the setup that comes next.

Match the section set to the doc type. A methodology writeup needs definitions, inputs, the calculation, results, validation. A proposal needs the ask, the evidence, the plan, the risks. A runbook needs setup, ordered steps, and the gotchas. Do not staple a section on because docs of this kind usually have one; every section must earn its place against the reader profile.

Keep it minimal. A section that does not move the reader from start state to end state does not belong. Fewer, load-bearing sections beat a complete-looking outline.

State the whole in one sentence before you lock the skeleton: the single thing this piece completes. If you cannot, the structure is not whole yet, and the skeleton is a pile of sections rather than a beginning, middle, and end.

Lay the sections out and reorder them before locking, principle 6. Sequencing problems show themselves only when you can see and move the pieces.

## Adversarial pass

Spawn parallel critics, each on a different failure mode:

1. **Prerequisite-order critic.** Walk the skeleton top to bottom. Does any section depend on a fact introduced later? Flag the forward reference and say where the fact should move.
2. **Dead-section critic.** For each section, name the job it does for the reader. If you cannot tie it to the end state in the profile, flag it for cutting.
3. **Doc-type-fit critic.** Does the section set fit what this reader expects of this kind of doc? Flag a missing load-bearing section (a proposal with no risks) and a stapled-on one (a methodology with a roadmap the reader did not ask for).
4. **Burial critic.** Is the thing the reader most needs near the top, or buried under setup? Flag a result or ask that sits too late.
5. **Wholeness critic.** Can you name the single action this skeleton completes, and does the last section answer the question the first one raises? Flag a skeleton with no end, no beginning, or a set of sections that would survive reordering, since that is a pile, not a whole.
6. **Care critic** (for a piece meant to move a reader). Does the opening give the reader a reason to care before the mechanics start, or does setup come before stakes? Flag a skeleton that asks for patience before it earns it.

A synthesizer merges the flags. Fix the skeleton here. A skeleton that survives these critics is what the storyboard fills in.
