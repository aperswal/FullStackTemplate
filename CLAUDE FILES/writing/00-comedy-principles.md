# Comedy Principles

Ten rules for humor. This is a specialist lens, and it is opt-in. Most of what the pipeline writes should not be funny: a methodology doc, an HLD, a postmortem, a Slack ask all lose trust when they reach for jokes. Pull this set in only when levity is part of the job: a talk or seminar that wants to land lighter, copy with personality, a keynote, a lightly humorous internal post. When in doubt, leave it out.

When humor does belong, it rides on top of the other rules, it does not replace them. A joke that costs clarity is cut by the rule above the rules in `00-line-principles.md`. Two of these rules are not jokes at all and apply to any writing: specificity (rule 3) and economy (rule 4) are the line set restated, so they are already owned and not re-checked.

## 1. The rule of three: set it, confirm it, break it

"Establish, reinforce, surprise." — Tony Allen

Two parallel items build a pattern, the third betrays it. Keep the items grammatically parallel so the pattern registers, and land the surprise on the final word.

Don't: let the third item just continue the list; add a fourth; or telegraph the swerve.

Good:
- (Escalate to absurd) "He packed light: a toothbrush, a clean shirt, and a kayak."
- (Swerve the tone) "She loved three things: her garden, her grandkids, and watching her enemies fail."
- (Deflate) "The contract promised wealth, freedom, and a branded lanyard."

Bad:
- (No subversion) "a toothbrush, a clean shirt, and some socks." Accurate, not a joke.
- (Overstays) "a toothbrush, a shirt, socks, a kayak, a harp, and regret." The kayak landed; everything after buries it.
- (Telegraphed) "He packed the usual, plus something ridiculous: a kayak." Naming the joke kills it.

## 2. Misdirection: lead them one way, reveal the other

"Comedy is a distortion of what is happening." — Steve Martin

Write a setup that implies one reading, then reveal a second you hid in plain sight. The setup must honestly support both. Go from A to C and let the mind snap in the missing B. That snap is the laugh.

Don't: make the turn arbitrary; over-explain the pivot; or plant the second reading so plainly there is nothing to discover.

Good:
- (Reframe a word) "My doctor told me to watch my drinking, so now I drink in front of a mirror."
- (Reframe the situation) "I have my mother's eyes. She keeps asking for them back."
- (Hidden literal) "The sign said 'fine for parking,' so I parked. Turns out it wasn't a compliment."

Bad:
- (Non sequitur) "...watch my drinking. Anyway, have you seen my keys?" Nothing reframed.
- (Telegraphed) spelling out the second meaning before the turn.
- (Over-explained) walking through both meanings until the snap is smothered.

## 3. Be specific

The line set's "be concrete" rule (`00-line-principles.md` #6), already owned. The comedy-specific twist: specificity is a route to a laugh, the surprise hides inside the detail, and you sharpen the funny noun, not the setup. "A damp Pomeranian named Gary," not "a dog."

## 4. Economy

The line set's "omit needless words" (`00-line-principles.md` #1), already owned. The comedy-specific twist: end on the funniest word and stop the instant it lands. A trailing tag that explains the joke kills it.

## 5. Word choice and sound

"Words with a 'k' in it are funny." — Neil Simon

When two words mean the same, pick the one that sounds funnier: short, with a hard plosive (k, g, p, b, t, d). Place the crunchy word on the punch, where the ear catches it.

Don't: choose the smooth abstract synonym; waste your funniest word in the setup; or end on a soft, vowel-heavy word.

Good:
- (Hard consonant on the punch) "He didn't quit. He absconded, to Kalamazoo."
- (Crunchy concrete noun) "Don't bring a side dish. Bring a bucket of pickles."
- (Funny-sounding specific) "My life coach is a parrot named Gary."

Bad:
- (Smooth synonym) "He relocated to a nearby municipality." Flat and airless.
- (Funny word wasted early) the kumquat is gone before the punch arrives.
- (Soft word on the punch) "Maybe some aioli." Mush where you needed a crack.

## 6. Deadpan and the pause

"The humorous story is told gravely." — Mark Twain

Write the absurd line to be played dead straight, no wink, no exclamation begging for the laugh. Let the character treat the ridiculous as ordinary. Hold the reveal with a beat, a line break or ellipsis, so the audience lands a half-second before the words finish. Twain called the pause the most important tool there is.

Don't: have the character flag the joke or laugh at it; rush the pause; or over-punctuate.

Good:
- (Absurd as mundane) "I lost my job, my apartment, and my left shoe. The shoe really stung."
- (Beat before the reveal) "I told her I'd love her forever. (beat) Forever ended in March."
- (Oblivious) "I'm an excellent driver. The three lawsuits are unrelated."

Bad:
- (Self-signaling) "Okay this part's hilarious, can you believe it?!" Begging.
- (No pause) the swerve with no room to breathe.
- (Over-punctuated) "EXCELLENT driver!!! wink." The deadpan is dead.

## 7. Callbacks: plant it early, detonate it late

A callback is comedy's Chekhov's gun, and it is the comic use of craft principle 7. Drop a specific, memorable image early and in passing. Later, at a high point, bring it back in a new context so the old laugh and the new surprise stack. Space them far enough apart that the audience half-forgets. Make sure the callback still reads for anyone who missed the seed.

Don't: call back a line that died; repeat it verbatim with no new angle; fire it so soon there is no forgetting gap; or explain the reference.

Good:
- (Escalated context) Early "he laminates his grocery lists," later "We found his will. Laminated."
- (Recontextualized) "we'll fix it in post" returning in the wedding vows.
- (Evolving gag) "Gary the parrot, my life coach," later "Gary has filed a complaint with HR."

Bad:
- (Calling back a dud) repeating a line that got silence, now twice as flat.
- (Verbatim) "Also, I hate cilantro." Nothing added.
- (Fired too soon) calling back two sentences later, no gap, no payoff.

## 8. Heightening and the game

"A consistent pattern of behavior that breaks from the everyday pattern." — Will Hines, on the game of the scene

Find the one funny idea, the first unusual thing, and commit. Then escalate in steps, each beat bigger than the last, while keeping the world believable in between. Justify, then heighten. Climb a staircase, do not take the elevator to the top.

Don't: throw a new unrelated joke every line; jump straight to the most extreme version; or heighten without grounding.

Good:
- (Stepwise) "basically a doctor": read a pamphlet, advises a stranger, scrubs in, redraws the org chart.
- (Same game, new arena) the same guy in a courtroom, "basically a lawyer."
- (Grounded) between leaps everyone reacts like it is normal, which earns the next beat.

Bad:
- (No through-line) doctor joke, then an unrelated airplane joke, then a cat joke.
- (Maxed out instantly) opening on "open-heart surgery in this Wendy's," no staircase left.
- (Ungrounded) escalations with no one reacting, so each jump feels arbitrary.

## 9. Yes, and

"Always agree and say yes... not only yes, but YES, AND." — Tina Fey

In a two-hander, each character accepts the reality the other sets, then adds something new. Agreement keeps the scene alive; the "and" compounds the comedy. Make statements, not questions; questions stall and push the work back on the partner. This one is live and interactive, so it applies to improvised or co-written scenes, not solo prose.

Don't: deny the premise; agree and add nothing; or interrogate in place of contributing.

Good:
- (Accept and add) A: "The baby's been forging my signature." B: "I know, the bank called about the yacht."
- (Make a statement) A: "We're clearly lost." B: "And we've been walking inside this dog's mouth for an hour."

Bad:
- (Blocking) "No you don't, that's your hand." Scene over.
- (Add nothing) "Yeah, it is." Dead air.
- (Question dump) "Are we? Where are we? Whose idea was this?" All stall, no contribution.

## 10. Truth and pain

"Comedy is tragedy plus time." — Carol Burnett

Pull material from real, specific, slightly painful truth: the embarrassing, the petty, the things people think but will not say. Then create distance so the audience can laugh without flinching: exaggeration, time, a character standing in for you, or making it happen to someone else. The laugh is recognition, "it's not just me."

Don't: reach for material so raw there is no distance yet; go so safe there is no truth to recognize; or manufacture distance by punching down, because cruelty is not perspective.

Good:
- (Recognizable, exaggerated) "I clean the house before the cleaner arrives. I don't want her to know who I really am."
- (Distance via a character) a pompous man's perfect day collapses step by step; we laugh because it is him.
- (Tragedy plus time) "I cried for a week when I got dumped at 19. Now it's my best material."

Bad:
- (Too raw) a joke about a named tragedy told the day it happened. Lands as a wince.
- (No truth) "Airplane food, am I right?" with no specific observation under it.
- (Punching down) the only distance is mocking someone powerless; the room recoils.
