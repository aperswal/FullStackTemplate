# Screen Principles

Ten rules from screenwriting. They are the narrative and persuasion lens. They bite hardest on anything with an audience and an arc: a talk, a narrative six-pager, a PR/FAQ, a launch story, a retrospective people will read start to finish. They bite least on a reference doc someone greps for one fact. Use them when the reader experiences the piece in order and you want them to feel the shape, not just retrieve from it.

Some of these restate the planning set in `00-craft-principles.md` from the screen angle. The map below says which add something new and which deepen a rule you already have, so the pipeline does not check the same thing twice under two names.

## 1. Structure is everything

"Screenplays are structure." — William Goldman

Do: Decide the sequence of cause and effect before polishing a line. Build the spine and protect it. Make every scene change the situation so the story moves on rails of "because of that."

Don't: Lead with clever lines or atmosphere and hope a story emerges. Don't let scenes sit in arbitrary order. Don't mistake beautiful writing for a working whole.

Good:
- (Cause-and-effect chain) In Toy Story every beat forces the next; remove one link and the chain collapses.
- (Time-lock) Die Hard bolts the story to one building and one night, so every scene escalates toward a fixed deadline.
- (Non-linear but rigid) Memento runs backward, yet the causal logic is airtight; the order is a delivery device, not chaos.

Bad:
- (Episodic drift) A road trip whose scenes could be shuffled, because nothing in scene 3 is caused by scene 2.
- (Dialogue-first, structure-never) Ninety pages of witty banter where nothing is at stake and no event changes the situation.
- (Saggy middle) A strong setup and climax bridged by a structureless second act; the lead wanders and the audience leaves.

## 2. Give every character a want

"Every character should want something, even if it is only a glass of water." — Kurt Vonnegut

For a doc, the "characters" are the sections and the actors in the story. Every section wants to move the reader somewhere; every person in a narrative wants something concrete in the scene they appear.

Do: Hand every section a concrete objective. The want gives it a reason to exist and a direction. Wants can be tiny or large; what matters is specific and active.

Don't: Write a section that only conveys information and pursues nothing. Don't keep the want vague when a concrete proxy is playable.

Good:
- (Tiny want, whole scene) A character racing to finish a call before the battery dies; small stake, total focus.
- (External vs internal) In Little Miss Sunshine Olive wants to win the pageant while the family needs to reconnect; both run at once.
- (Antagonist with an equal want) A villain who believes he is the hero, his want as urgent and specific as the lead's.

Bad:
- (No want) A lead who only has things happen to them for two acts.
- (Vague want) "He wants to find himself." Nothing concrete to chase or track.
- (Furniture) A friend who appears only to ask "so what will you do?", a prompt with legs.

## 3. Drama is intention meeting obstacle

"Somebody wants something. Something's standing in their way of getting it." — Aaron Sorkin

Do: Put a formidable obstacle in front of the want; the collision is the drama. Make the intention urgent and the obstacle genuinely hard. The harder the obstacle, the stronger the scene.

Don't: Let the want be met easily. Don't write conflict-free scenes where everyone agrees. Don't make the obstacle a pushover.

Good:
- (External) A climber wants the summit before a storm; the mountain and weather are the wall.
- (Clash of equals) In A Few Good Men the lawyer wants the truth and a powerful colonel stands in the way; two strong wills.
- (Internal) Fear, pride, or addiction blocks the character; the fight is with the self.

Bad:
- (No obstacle) The hero wants the job and is handed it.
- (Trivial, instant fix) A problem raised and resolved in a page at no cost.
- (Idiot plot) The only obstacle is two people refusing one honest conversation.

## 4. Character is revealed by choice under pressure

"True character is revealed in the choices a human being makes under pressure." — Robert McKee

Story-first. In a narrative doc this is how a person in the story earns the reader's read of them; in most non-fiction it is light.

Do: Force a choice between two meaningful options under heat. The greater the pressure, the deeper the revelation. Let actions under stress define character, not labels.

Don't: Tell us a trait, prove it. Don't give only cost-free decisions. Don't resolve the pressure with luck.

Good:
- (Sacrifice) Save themselves or someone else, not both.
- (Two goods or two evils) A parent choosing which valid loyalty to honor.
- (Against the stated self) The "tough" character who quietly does the kind thing.

Bad:
- (Telling) "I'd do anything for my family," never tested.
- (Pressure-free) A decision that costs nothing reveals nothing.
- (Deus ex machina) An outside force solves it just as the choice arrives, stealing the meaning.

## 5. Cut anything that isn't drama

"Who wants what? What happens if they don't get it? Why now?" — David Mamet

The three questions test every scene. The first two echo wants and obstacles. "Why now" is new and sharp: a scene with no reason to happen at this moment is a scene that can move or vanish.

Do: Interrogate every section with the three questions. If a section only conveys information, give it conflict or cut it. Enter late, leave early.

Don't: Dress an exposition dump as a scene. Don't keep a section because the writing is nice. Don't mistake "clear" for "compelling."

Good:
- (Exposition inside conflict) Two agents argue about how to do the mission; we absorb the facts and feel the friction.
- (Enter late) A negotiation that opens mid-argument, already hot.
- (Leave early) Cut the instant the point lands, trusting the reader to bridge.

Bad:
- (Info-dump) A section whose only job is to brief us on what we could have learned in motion.
- (Slow ramp) A page of arrivals and coffee before anything happens.
- (Overstaying) A section that hits its point and lingers three exchanges longer.

## 6. Show, don't tell

"Let the audience add up two plus two." — Billy Wilder, crediting Ernst Lubitsch

Central for docs. Give the reader the evidence and let them reach the conclusion; do not hand them the conclusion and ask them to trust it.

Do: Convey the point through specifics and action, then let the reader do the arithmetic. Give them "2 + 2" and trust them to reach "4."

Don't: Spell out what the evidence already shows. Don't state a feeling or a judgment the facts already carry. Don't hand the reader "4."

Good:
- (Revealed through action) The opening of Up tells an entire marriage with almost no dialogue.
- (Status shown by behavior) A new boss silently moving a nameplate off a desk; we grasp the power shift wordlessly.
- (Inferred from environment) A messy apartment with an unopened divorce envelope; we infer the whole life.

Bad:
- (On the nose) "I'm so angry because you abandoned me as a child." Stating the subtext kills it.
- (Redundant narration) "It was raining" over a shot of rain.
- (Emotion announced) "I feel so alone" instead of a figure apart at a crowded table.

## 7. Suspense beats surprise

"Whenever possible the public must be informed." — Alfred Hitchcock

Partly new for docs: front-load the stakes so the reader feels the weight of what follows, rather than springing it at the end.

Do: Give the reader knowledge that creates tension as they read on. Show the bomb under the table, then the ordinary scene becomes unbearable. Sustained tension lasts longer than a single jolt.

Don't: Hide everything for one reveal when sustained pressure would be richer. Don't waste a real threat by springing it with no buildup. The exception is when the twist itself is the whole point.

Good:
- (Ticking bomb) We know it detonates at 1:00; the characters chat; every second is agony.
- (We know, they don't) A character accepts a ride from someone we know is the killer.
- (The secret that will detonate the room) At dinner we know one spouse is unfaithful and read every loaded line.

Bad:
- (All surprise) The bomb explodes from nowhere; ten seconds of shock where minutes of dread were available.
- (Withholding for a cheat) Hiding what a character clearly knew, to fool the reader, then a gotcha.
- (Telegraphed, no tension) The bomb shown with no clock and no stakes.

## 8. Set up and pay off

The rifle on the wall in act one must fire by the last. — Chekhov, paraphrased.

Same rule as planning principle 7. Do not run a separate check for it; the storyboard plans plants and payoffs and the review verifies them. Listed here only so the screen set is complete.

## 9. Make the audience care, fast

"Make me care." — Andrew Stanton

New and early: earn investment before you ask the reader to follow mechanics. The doc version is leading with why the reader should care before the machinery.

Do: Give the reader a reason to care early. Open on the stakes, the problem that bites them, or the result that matters, before the setup and the method.

Don't: Launch into mechanics before the reader has a reason to follow. Don't open with an opaque setup and expect patience. Don't confuse what is fun to write with what makes a reader care.

Good:
- (Stakes first) A doc opens on the cost of the thing being broken, so the reader wants the fix that follows.
- (Empathy through hardship) A retrospective opens on the on-call night that went wrong; we are with the team before the analysis.
- (Hook through the win) A launch story opens on the outcome, so the reader follows the how.

Bad:
- (Mechanics first) Ten minutes of setup before the reader knows why any of it matters.
- (No window in) An opaque opening with nothing to hold onto.
- (Generic) A start so vague there is nothing to care about.

## 10. Keep the protagonist active

"You admire a character for trying more than for their successes." — Emma Coats, Pixar

Story-first; light for most non-fiction. In a narrative doc, the team or the system is the protagonist, and the reader bonds with the effort, the failures, the trying again.

Do: Let the protagonist drive the story through choices and action, failing and trying again. We bond with struggle, not effortless wins.

Don't: Let the hero be a passenger who is rescued. Don't make success easy or given. Don't have them react for two acts and act only at the end.

Good:
- (Trying, failing, trying) A character who keeps attempting the hard thing and getting back up.
- (Driving through choices) The protagonist's own decisions cause each turn; they are the engine, not the cargo.
- (Earning the win) The climax is won by something learned across the story, not by luck.

Bad:
- (Passenger) Events happen to the lead; others solve the problems.
- (Rescued at the climax) A coincidence saves the hero at the crucial moment. A coincidence that creates trouble is fair; one that solves it is cheating.
- (Effortless success) The goal achieved with no struggle, so nothing to admire.
