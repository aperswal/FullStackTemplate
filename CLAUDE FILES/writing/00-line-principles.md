# Line Principles

Ten rules for the sentence and the word. This is the line-edit lens, and it belongs to Stage 4 (drafting) and Stage 5 (review). Where the planning and screen sets decide what the piece says and in what order, these decide how each sentence reads.

Much of this overlaps `00-voice-reference.md`, which already bans the inflated words, counts the sentence lengths, and holds the example bank. The map below says which rules the voice reference already enforces and which add something new. The new part of almost every rule here is the "when it flips" clause: the voice reference states rules flat, this set says when each one yields. Those exceptions are the reason to keep this file.

## 1. Omit needless words

"Omit needless words." — Strunk and White

Do: Make every word earn its place. If a word can go without changing the meaning, cut it.
Don't: Pad with redundancies, wind-ups, wordy phrases, and hedges that add length but no meaning.

- (Redundancy) "She returned back to the office to collaborate together with her team" becomes "She returned to the office to work with her team."
- (Wordy phrase) "At this point in time, we are in the process of making a decision" becomes "We are deciding now."
- (Throat-clearing) "It is important to note that the results were inconclusive" becomes "The results were inconclusive."
- (Hedge pile-up) "I would just like to sort of suggest that we might perhaps consider a change" becomes "We should consider a change."

When it flips: Brevity cuts dead words, not detail the reader needs. Do not shorten a sentence by deleting the fact that informs it.

## 2. Use plain, short words

"Never use a long word where a short one will do." — Orwell

Do: Prefer the short, common word.
Don't: Reach for Latinate, corporate, or academic diction to sound important. Worst, do not use inflated language to hide what you mean.

- (Inflated verb) "We will endeavor to facilitate the utilization of the new system" becomes "We will help you use the new system."
- (Buzzword) "Let's leverage our core competencies to action the deliverables" becomes "Let's use what we're good at to finish the work."
- (Latinate) "The committee will commence deliberations regarding the matter" becomes "The committee will start discussing it."
- (Euphemism that hides) "Headcount was right-sized in response to negative growth" becomes "We laid off staff because sales fell."

When it flips: A long word is right when it is the precise word and no short word means the same. Plainness is about not inflating, not dumbing down.

## 3. Find the exact word

"The difference between the lightning-bug and the lightning." — Mark Twain

Do: Choose the word that says exactly what you mean, including the right degree and connotation.
Don't: Settle for the near-cousin word or a thesaurus synonym that quietly shifts the meaning.

- (Vague to precise verb) "The economy was affected by the policy" becomes "The policy crippled the economy."
- (Wrong register) "The CEO was utilized as a scapegoat" becomes "The CEO was used as a scapegoat."
- (Near-miss adjective) "It was a continuous problem" becomes "It was a continual problem" (continuous is unbroken, continual is recurring).
- (Empty intensifier to exact word) "The reaction was very bad" becomes "The reaction was hostile."

When it flips: Precision sometimes needs the longer or rarer word. That is the moment rule 2's short-word preference yields to exactness.

## 4. Use strong verbs, cut adverbs

"The road to hell is paved with adverbs." — Stephen King

Do: Let a strong, specific verb do the work. If the verb is right, the adverb is redundant.
Don't: Glue "-ly" adverbs onto weak verbs to manufacture force.

- (Adverb duplicates verb) "She whispered quietly" becomes "She whispered."
- (Adverb props a weak verb) "He walked quickly to the door" becomes "He strode to the door."
- (Adverb in attribution) "'Get out,' he said angrily" becomes "'Get out,' he snapped."
- (Intensifier) "The findings were really very significant" becomes "The findings were significant."

When it flips: Keep the adverb when it changes the verb's meaning instead of repeating it. "She smiled sadly" reverses the default sense of "smiled," so it earns its place. The test: does the adverb add information or echo what is already there.

## 5. Prefer the active voice

"Activate your verbs." — Roy Peter Clark

Do: Put the actor before the action. Active sentences are shorter, clearer, accountable.
Don't: Hide the actor behind a passive construction, especially to dodge responsibility.

- (Dodging blame) "Mistakes were made" becomes "We made mistakes."
- (Bureaucratic) "It was decided that the project would be cancelled" becomes "The board cancelled the project."
- (Limp by-phrase) "The election was won by the challenger" becomes "The challenger won the election."

When it flips: Use passive on purpose when the receiver is the real subject, or the actor is unknown or irrelevant. "The suspect was arrested at dawn" rightly spotlights the suspect. "The bridge was built in 1890" needs no builder. Passive by choice is a tool; passive by accident is fog.

## 6. Be concrete and specific

"Not car, but Cadillac." — Natalie Goldberg

Do: Name the particular thing. Specific, sensory detail is what the reader sees and remembers.
Don't: Default to generic category nouns and abstractions: things, issues, stuff, very nice.

- (Generic to specific noun) "She drove an old car" becomes "She drove a rusted '78 Cadillac."
- (Abstraction to image) "The neighborhood was poor" becomes "Plywood covered three windows on the block."
- (Vague quantity to number) "Sales dropped a lot" becomes "Sales dropped 38% in one quarter."
- (Empty praise to concrete reason) "The hotel was lovely" becomes "The hotel left chocolates on the pillow, and the bath ran hot in seconds."

When it flips: Do not bury the reader in detail. Select the telling particular; one vivid specific beats five vague ones.

## 7. Revise relentlessly, cut what you love

"Murder your darlings." — Arthur Quiller-Couch

Do: Treat the first draft as raw material. Cut even the clever line you are proud of if it does not serve the reader.
Don't: Protect a beautiful sentence that distracts from the point, or ship a draft you have not rewritten.

- (Purple show-off) "Quarterly revenue, that fickle mistress who dances just beyond the CFO's trembling fingertips, rose 4%" becomes "Revenue rose 4%."
- (Cliched opener) "Since the dawn of human civilization, people have always needed to communicate" becomes "We've always needed to talk to each other," or cut it and start at the real point.
- (Joke fighting the tone) "My grandfather, who never met a casserole he didn't fear, taught me to fish" becomes "My grandfather taught me to fish."
- (Flat to punchy) "The meeting was about the budget and it went on for a while and we didn't decide much" becomes "The budget meeting ran two hours and settled nothing."

When it flips: Keep a darlings file. A line cut from one piece often fits a future one. You are not destroying the writing, you are moving it out of the reader's way.

## 8. Write in your own honest voice

"Don't alter your voice to fit the subject." — William Zinsser

Do: Sound like a person, not an institution. Develop one recognizable voice and keep it.
Don't: Hide behind stiff official prose, imitate a generic authoritative tone, or over-hedge until no person is audible.

- (Institutional to human) "It is the position of this office that the request cannot be accommodated" becomes "We can't do this, and here's why."
- (Fake formality) "One must endeavor to ascertain the facts prior to forming conclusions" becomes "Get the facts before you decide."
- (Borrowed pomp) "Heretofore, the undersigned posits the following contention" becomes "Here's what I think."
- (Timid hedging) "This may possibly be, in some sense, arguably a small concern" becomes "This worries me."

When it flips: Register shifts with the occasion; the same writer is funny in a blog and sober in an obituary. What stays constant is the underlying voice, your way of thinking and phrasing, recognizable across both.

## 9. Open with a lead that makes a promise

"A lead is a promise." — John McPhee

Do: Make the first sentence pull the reader in and signal what is coming. The lead is a door, not a throat-clear.
Don't: Bury the point under background, definitions, or "in today's world" wind-ups.

- (Buried lede) A council recap that opens on the pledge of allegiance and reaches the 40% water-rate hike in paragraph five becomes "Your water bill is about to jump 40%."
- (Background dump) "This report will discuss several factors that may be related to customer churn" becomes "We lose a customer every nine minutes. Here's why."
- (Generic wind-up) "In today's fast-paced world, communication is more important than ever" becomes "I sent the wrong email to 4,000 people. Here's what it taught me."

When it flips: A lead need not shout. A quiet, specific opening can promise as powerfully as a punchy one, "The river was four feet higher than the day before," as long as it is true to what follows. The non-negotiable: whatever the lead promises, the piece must deliver. This is the line-level partner of the screen "make me care" rule.

## 10. Vary sentence length for rhythm, read it aloud

"This sentence has five words." — Gary Provost

A run of equal-length sentences drones. Varying the length creates music.

Do: Mix short, medium, and long sentences so the rhythm carries the reader. Read every draft aloud to hear the music and the stumbles.
Don't: String together sentences of the same length and shape until the prose flatlines. Never check by ear alone after only reading silently.

- (All short, monotone) "The plan failed. We lost money. The team quit. I went home. Nothing worked." becomes "The plan failed. We lost money, the team walked out, and by the time I got home that night, one thing was clear: nothing we built would ever work."
- (All long, drowning) A single coast-to-coast-lawyers sentence that never breathes becomes "The deal took months. Lawyers flew coast to coast, revising terms until everyone was exhausted. Then the buyer changed his mind. It was over."
- (Run-on, no breath) "Revenue grew but costs grew faster which meant margins shrank and the board got nervous..." becomes "Revenue grew. Costs grew faster, so margins shrank. The board got nervous and asked questions we couldn't answer. The meeting ran long."

When it flips: Match rhythm to content. Short, clipped sentences create tension and speed. One long flowing sentence builds and crests. The wrong rhythm in the wrong place is the only mistake.

## The one rule above the rules

Orwell closed his own list by saying you should break any rule before writing anything outright clumsy. Every writer here would agree. These are tools for clarity and for the reader. When a rule fights clarity, clarity wins. Learn the rules well enough to know exactly what you give up when you break one, then break it on purpose.
