# Research and Evidence Synthesis

Read together with `core.md`. These rules cover how to investigate a question rigorously and synthesize a body of evidence — literature reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and any work that gathers, evaluates, and combines sources to reach a defensible conclusion. Apply them whenever a task involves research, evidence gathering, or synthesizing prior work rather than writing software.

This is a deduplicated synthesis of research-methodology and evidence-synthesis practice. It is a domain file alongside `ux.md`; where it touches discovery interviewing or note synthesis, `ux.md` covers the product-research variant and this file covers the academic/evidence-review variant.

## Framing the Question

- **Turn a broad topic into a single, answerable research question first,** because everything downstream — search, screening, analysis — depends on what you're actually trying to find out.
- **State the question in structured terms** — population, intervention or exposure, comparison, outcome — so it can guide your search and screening precisely.
- **Decide what kind of review the question needs before starting:** a quick traditional review, a scoping or mapping review, a full systematic review, or a meta-analysis. Each demands different rigor and effort, and matching the type to the question is the first design decision.
- **Treat the review itself as a research project** with its own design, sampling, and threats to validity — not as a casual summary.
- **Position your question against what already exists,** identifying the gap or tension you're addressing rather than restating prior work.

## Protocol and Documentation

- **Write a protocol up front** specifying the question, search strategy, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and analysis plan, so the process is repeatable and defensible.
- **Document every decision as you go,** so someone else could reproduce your search and reach the same set of sources.
- **Report your method transparently enough that a reader can judge and reproduce it,** including what you searched, what you included, and how you analyzed it. Use a flow diagram to show how you got from all candidate sources to the final included set.

## Searching

- **Search systematically across multiple sources** rather than relying on what you already know or what's easy to find. Build an explicit search strategy with defined keywords, databases, and date ranges instead of ad hoc browsing.
- **Treat source types as distinct,** each with different strengths: internal records, government statistics, syndicated and commercial data, archives, and published literature.
- **Keep searching until new sources stop adding anything new,** and record what you searched and what you found.
- **Trace findings back to the original source** rather than citing secondhand reports of them.
- **For datasets, confirm you can actually obtain and use the data** — government data, survey archives, administrative records — before building on its existence.

## Screening and Source Evaluation

- **Apply inclusion and exclusion criteria consistently to every candidate,** and log why each source was kept or dropped.
- **Screen in stages** — title, then abstract, then full text — to handle large volumes without bias.
- **Interrogate every secondary source:** why it was originally produced, who produced it, what was actually measured, when, and how. Data collected for someone else's purpose may not fit yours, so check definitions, coverage, timing, and units before trusting it.
- **Assess each study's risk of bias systematically using explicit criteria,** not gut feeling. Separate the quality of a source from the convenience of a source — recency and authority do not guarantee fitness for your question.
- **Cross-check a finding against independent sources before relying on it.**

## Extraction and Synthesis

- **Extract data into a structured, consistent format** so studies can be compared on the same terms, and code study characteristics — design, sample, methods, context — so you can later test whether they explain differences in results.
- **Synthesize rather than summarize:** map the field, surface the key debates, and show how the evidence fits together. Organize the review around ideas and arguments, not one source per paragraph.
- **Build an argument through claim, reasons, and evidence,** anticipating and answering the strongest objections. Separate evidence from claims, and don't let a strong assertion stand in for data.

## Quantitative Pooling (Meta-Analysis)

- **When studies are comparable, pool them quantitatively using effect sizes** rather than counting how many were statistically significant — vote counting is misleading.
- **Convert each result to a common effect size and weight studies by their precision** rather than treating them equally.
- **Choose a fixed-effect model only if the studies estimate the same true effect;** use a random-effects model when they don't.
- **Measure and report heterogeneity,** and investigate its sources with subgroup analysis or meta-regression instead of ignoring it.
- **Check for publication bias** with funnel plots and sensitivity analyses, and treat a suspiciously tidy result as a reason for caution rather than comfort.

## Validity, Limits, and Ethics

- **Identify the threats to validity at each stage** — problem definition, search, evaluation, analysis, interpretation — and address them deliberately.
- **Make the limits of your synthesis explicit,** acknowledging what the existing literature cannot tell you.
- **Respect ethical and licensing constraints on reusing data and sources,** and represent others' findings accurately and fairly.
